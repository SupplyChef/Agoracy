# Agoracy: writing guide

Read this before drafting, editing or reviewing anything in this repo. It records how RJ wants the work done.

## Audience

Write for a skeptical legislator in another state who will vote on something similar. They are smart, busy and suspicious of advocacy from either side. Every sentence should help them understand what happened and judge it for themselves.

## Voice: write like John Carreyrou

- Tell it as a story built from documents. Open on a concrete, verifiable fact, not a thesis.
- Use short declarative sentences and plain words. Let the facts carry the weight; no adjectives doing the arguing.
- Keep it chronological. Introduce things in the order they happened, and make the opening example part of that order. Don't jump between threads.
- Link each step to the one before with transitions ("That same year", "Then the courts stepped in").
- End by returning to the opening and naming the open question the next post answers.
- Never invent scenes, dialogue, thoughts or quotes. Every detail must come from a record.

## Edit like a New York Times editor

- Give each post a headline and a one-line summary.
- Attribute every number in the sentence where it appears ("according to the state's auditor general"). The source list is backup, not a substitute.
- Give both sides through named, on-the-record voices making their real argument, not a boast. A court majority and its dissent; a bill's sponsor and its opponents. Generic "supporters say / critics say" lines are allowed only as a short closing summary after the named voices.
- Watch for framing that tilts. A dramatic contrast, such as a pilot's cost against a mature program's cost, needs a fair measure of scale right after it.
- Use exact words. "Worth" or "funding", not "paying", when part of the money is tax credits. "Open to every child", not "serves every family".
- Check internal consistency. Nothing may contradict another line in the post or an earlier post.
- Courtesy titles (Gov., Sen., Chief Justice). Spell out "percent". Explain jargon the out-of-state reader won't know (for example, Florida calls vouchers "scholarships").

## Verify every claim

- Use primary sources: statutes and enacted laws, court opinions, legislative staff analyses and roll calls, state auditors (OPPAGA, the Auditor General), state agency data, federal data (NCES), and official statements for quotes.
- Advocacy groups (on any side) and news stories are not enough by themselves. Cite them only for what they said, or to point to a primary record.
- Check quotes word for word against the original.
- Check arithmetic and show it in the fact-check table (sums, ratios, averages).
- Prefer the enacted law to a bill summary when a date or rule could be challenged. Give chapter numbers.
- If a claim can't be confirmed in a primary source, cut it or move it to the notes with a caveat. Don't infer and present the inference as fact.
- Say whether dollars are nominal or adjusted for inflation.

## What goes in the main text

Keep a statement in the main text only if the reader needs it to follow this post's main point or to weigh the two sides. Everything else goes in the notes, even if it is true and interesting. Typical moves to the notes:
- accounting nuances that belong to a later post's topic
- secondary counts and breakdowns
- a second quote that repeats a point already made
- background that doesn't change the reader's understanding

## Format of a post file

Each post lives at `series/<series>/<NN>-<slug>/post.md`, with its image beside it as `image.png`. The file has these parts, in order:

1. **Front matter:** title, series, number, status, image, updated.
2. **Post text (paste as is):** series tag ("Florida vouchers, 1 of 12"), headline, summary line, body, and "Sources are in the first comment."
3. **First comment (paste as is):** numbered sources with links, each saying what it supports.
4. **Fact-check table:** every claim in the post, its source, and what the source says (verbatim where possible).
5. **Notes:** accurate, sourced details kept out of the main text, for replying to comments or for later posts.
6. **Editor's notes:** what changed in each draft and why.

## Facebook conventions

- About 500 to 800 words. The first two or three lines must work on their own before "See more".
- No bold in the post text. Plain paragraphs; section labels in capitals if needed.
- Links go in the first comment, not the post.
- One 1080×1080 image per post, readable on a phone, with its sources printed on it. Image numbers must match the post text.

## Process

1. Draft from verified sources.
2. Edit as a Times editor and fix what you find.
3. Review as a critical reader from each side and ask whether the post makes its point convincingly. Discuss the proposed changes with RJ before making them.
4. Keep the fact-check table and editor's notes current with every draft. On request, provide a diff against the previous draft.
