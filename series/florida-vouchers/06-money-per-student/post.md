Florida's Public Schools Now Lean on Rising Property Values

Since 2019, vouchers have taken almost all new state money for Florida's schools. Public schools held about even because property taxes rose. That works only while property values keep rising.

Florida pays for public schools with state money and local property taxes. Each year, lawmakers set the total and how much property taxes must raise. The state pays the rest. Vouchers come out of the state's part.

From 2019-20 to 2024-25, the state added $3.1 billion for schools. Vouchers took $2.8 billion of it, according to the Department of Education. The state's money for public schools barely grew. Prices rose by about a quarter. The number of public school students stayed about the same.

Property taxes made up most of the gap. Property values rose by about two-thirds. Lawmakers cut the school tax rate, but by less than values rose, so property taxes brought in much more. By 2024-25, they paid more than half of public schools' money. In 2019-20, they had paid less than half.

After inflation, money per public school student fell about 5 percent, by our calculation from state and federal data. Most of the drop came by 2022-23, when inflation was high. Vouchers were not yet open to every child.

This has limits. If property values stop rising, lawmakers must add state money or let money per student fall. When money runs short during a year, the law pays vouchers in full and cuts districts, Senate staff wrote in 2025. Charter schools are public schools in Florida, so they are cut along with their districts.

This year, Gov. Ron DeSantis pushed an amendment to cut homeowners' property taxes. Lawmakers took school taxes out of it. Voters approved it on Nov. 3.

Sen. Danny Burgess, a Zephyrhills Republican who leads the Senate's school budget committee, said in May that the new budget "increases per student funding by $150." That figure is not adjusted for inflation. Andrew Spar, who heads the state teachers union, said in 2025: "Draining dollars out of public schools leaves our public schools short."

In 2019-20, the state paid most of the cost of public schools. Now property owners pay more than half. The next post asks what this means for a school district, and whether it can cut costs when students leave.

Sources are in the first comment.

## Notes: details kept out of the main text

- **2007-08 (in draft 1's text, cut in draft 2).** About $10,500 per student in 2024-25 dollars, state 51 percent and local 49 percent (table above). The required local effort was $7,902,582,747 at 4.843 mills, about the same in dollars as 2019-20 (2007-08 Final Calculation, https://www.fldoe.org/file/7507/0077043-07-08fefp-finalcalc-1.pdf). Enrollment 2,666,811 (NCES Digest 2012, Table 36). Money per student fell in the recession and had not recovered by 2019-20.
- **The school share of the amendment, had it applied.** Bloomberg reported that a fiscal analysis put the cut to school district property tax revenue at $3.4 billion in 2027 and $5.6 billion in 2028 if schools had been included. The primary analysis was not found; the House staff analysis of the enacted version estimates only non-school losses ($4.95 billion in 2027-28, $8.78 billion in 2028-29, $11.86 billion recurring). Use only after finding the primary estimate.
- **No court-set floor.** In Citizens for Strong Schools v. State Board of Education (Fla. Jan. 4, 2019, SC18-67), a divided court rejected a broad challenge to school funding under Article IX, section 1, holding that the plaintiffs had not offered a manageable standard; Justices Pariente and Lewis dissented. Wording and vote to confirm from the opinion before use.
- **2025-26 (preliminary, not in the text).** The 2025-26 Final Calculation (Oct. 5, 2026) shows Total State Funding $15,573,752,524, Family Empowerment adjustment $3,906,797,314, state left $11,666,955,210, Total Local Funding $13,658,001,644, required local effort $10,909,379,489 at 3.092 mills, taxable value $3,827,738,074,700. Local share 53.9 percent. With the Education Estimating Conference's public enrollment of about 2,790,700 (not yet an NCES count) and CPI-U (324.000+330.724)/2 = 327.36: state about $4,060, local about $4,750, total about $8,810 per student in 2024-25 dollars. Use once NCES publishes the 2025-26 count.
- **Why the Senate's per-student figure differs from ours.** The Senate's $9,337.67 is total FEFP funds divided by all students in the formula, which since 2023-24 include Family Empowerment scholarship students (2025-26 unweighted FTE: 3,178,422.96, against about 2.79 million public students). Ours removes voucher money and divides by public school students only. Both are nominal in the Senate's case.
- **The recession years.** Local money paid more than half of the formula in 2008-09 (52.6 percent) and 2009-10 (53.1 percent), when property tax collections lagged the fall in state revenue (project spreadsheet, "State vs local" sheet). Those years also had about $0.9 billion a year in federal stimulus money, excluded here. So 2023-24 is not the first year local money passed half.
- **Equalization.** The required local effort is part of each district's formula total, not added to it: a district with high property values raises more locally and gets less from the state. Local shares range from about 10 percent to 90 percent by district (FEA, "A Local Look at Property Taxes," Aug. 21, 2025, citing the FEFP). So a statewide shift toward property taxes does not by itself mean richer counties get more per student; the discretionary 0.748 mills is the exception. For post 7.
- **Homeowners' bills.** A lower rate on higher values does not tell us what any one owner paid. Homestead assessments are capped at 3 percent a year (Save Our Homes), so much of the growth falls on non-homestead and new property. Not checked in detail; keep out of the text.
- **Amendment 3 (November 2026).** Raises the homestead exemption for non-school levies and lowers the non-homestead assessment limit for non-school taxes. "School taxes would continue to be uncapped and based on Just/Market Value" (county property appraiser explainer, 2026). So it does not change school property taxes directly. Lawmakers "move[d] to protect schools" from Gov. DeSantis's broader plan in a June 1, 2026 special session (Tampa Bay Times caption). The full article was not retrieved.
- **Other measures.** NCES per-pupil spending (all funds, including federal and building money) would give a fuller picture and a national rank. Not used here; to add if a reader asks.
- **Discretionary supplement.** The 2025-26 and 2026-27 calculations include a "State-Funded Discretionary Supplement" ($862.9 million and $1.0 billion) inside Total State Funding, so it is counted in our state figures. What it pays for is not checked.
- **CPI method.** The project spreadsheet uses calendar-year averages and slightly different enrollment counts, so its figures differ by up to about $30 per student (for example, $10,501 vs $10,479 for 2007-08). The post uses the table above.
- **Andrew Spar's other line in the same article:** "to not give enough resources to our districts to make sure we're meeting the needs of students, is a failure." Not used; same point.
