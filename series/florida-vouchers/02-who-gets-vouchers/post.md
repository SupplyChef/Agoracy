Who Gets Florida's Vouchers, and Where Were They Before?

In their early years, Florida's vouchers went mostly to poor children leaving public schools. In the first year they were open to everyone, 69 percent of new recipients were already in private school.

In 2009, an economist hired by the state reported on the children using Florida's Tax Credit Scholarship. David N. Figlio, then at the University of Florida, compared the students who joined in 2007-08 with low-income children who stayed in public school. The scholarship students were poorer. About 81 percent qualified for free lunch, against 76 percent of those who stayed. They had scored lower on tests the year before, and they came disproportionately from low-rated schools.

That was by design. At the time, a child qualified only if the family was poor enough for free or reduced-price school lunch, and only if the child was leaving a public school or starting kindergarten or first grade, according to OPPAGA, the Legislature's research office.

In 2023, House Bill 1 opened both scholarship programs to every child eligible to attend a Florida public school, whatever the family's income. That included children already in private school.

Before the vote, House budget staff estimated what would follow. They counted about 116,600 private school students without a scholarship who would become eligible, assumed half would take one, and put the first-year number at 58,315. Students leaving public schools, the staff noted, were already counted in the school funding formula, so they added no new cost. Students already in private school did.

The first count came that September. Step Up For Students, the nonprofit that administers most of the scholarships, reported 122,895 new scholarship students. Of those, 84,505, or 69 percent, had already been in private school. Another 22,294 were starting kindergarten. Public schools supplied 16,096, or 13 percent.

Step Up also reported family income for all scholarship students that fall. Forty-four percent came from households below 185 percent of the federal poverty level, about $55,000 for a family of four. Twenty-seven percent came from households above 400 percent, about $120,000, or did not report their income.

The two sides read the numbers differently. Step Up asked whether 16,096 students represented "an 'exodus' from the public schools as critics forecast." The Florida Policy Institute, a nonprofit research group that opposed the law, said the "money is going to subsidize tuition that families were already paying."

The children who did leave public schools also looked different from the early recipients. A 2025 evaluation for the state by Florida State University's Learning Systems Institute compared new Family Empowerment students who had left public schools in 2023-24 with the students who stayed. Among the new scholarship students, 41.5 percent had qualified for free or reduced-price lunch, against 51.9 percent of those who stayed. More of them were white, more came from schools the state rated A, and their earlier test scores were about the same. The two studies used different comparison groups, because by 2023 every public school student was eligible.

Two years in, the enrollment counts point the same way, though they are measured differently. From 2022-23 to 2024-25, the number of scholarship students rose by 230,581, according to the state's auditor general. Over the same years, enrollment in Florida's private schools rose by 51,452, according to the Department of Education's annual survey. The scholarship count includes some students taught at home and anyone funded during the year, so the gap is only a rough guide. It does suggest that much of the growth came from families who were already paying for private school.

Supporters say families who pay school taxes should get the same help whatever school their children attended before. Critics say the state is now paying tuition it did not pay before, while moving relatively few children out of public schools.

In 2009, the typical new scholarship student was a poor child leaving a public school. By 2023, the typical new one was already in a private school. What that shift costs the state is the subject of a later post. The next post looks at the other ways Florida families leave district schools: charter schools and home education.

Sources are in the first comment.

**Caveats:**
- The 69 percent figure comes from Step Up For Students, which administers the scholarships and supports them, and reached us through a news report. No state agency has published the same breakdown that we found. The figures were not disputed by critics, who cited them.
- The Step Up count was taken Sept. 8, 2023, early in the school year. The House estimate covered the full year and only income-based scholarships, so the two are not strictly comparable; the post does not compare them directly.
- Whether the Auditor General's Tax Credit count for 2024-25 includes home-education (PEP) students is not stated in what we have read. The post says "some students taught at home" to stay safe. To confirm.
- "Typical" in the closing line rests on the 69 percent (2023) and on the early eligibility rule plus Figlio (2007-08).

## Notes: details kept out of the main text

- **When the prior-public-school rule ended.** OPPAGA documents it in 2008. Later laws loosened it before HB 1; the exact year is not yet verified. Before HB 1, the Family Empowerment rule barred students currently enrolled in public school, and students who spent the prior year in public school were not counted against the program's cap. (House PKA analysis pp. 10-11.)
- **Income limits just before HB 1.** By 2022-23, the Tax Credit and Family Empowerment income cap had risen to 400 percent of the poverty level ($111,000 for a family of four), with priority for families at or below 185 percent. (House PKA analysis pp. 3, 10-11.) HB 1 kept the 185 percent priority. Post 1 says the 2021 law set 375 percent; the adjusted cap reached 400 percent by 2022-23. Consistent, but worth knowing if asked.
- **Race in 2007-08.** New Tax Credit participants were more likely to be Black (46.9 vs 34.2 percent) and less likely to be Hispanic or white. (Figlio 2009, Table IV.1.) In 2023-24, new Family Empowerment students from public schools were 18.7 percent Black vs 20.1 percent of those who stayed; 32.6 vs 37.8 percent Hispanic. (FSU LSI, Figure 6.)
- **English learners.** 15.3 percent of new Family Empowerment students from public schools vs 23.7 percent of those who stayed. (FSU LSI, Figure 6.)
- **Scale, fall 2023.** 242,929 students using Tax Credit and Family Empowerment scholarships in 2,098 private schools as of Sept. 8, 2023; 15,097 home-education (PEP) students. (Step Up, via News Service of Florida.)
- **Cost estimates in 2023.** The House's final analysis put the bill's first-year state cost at $209.6 million. FPI estimated $676 million of the expansion would go to students already in private school, and Step Up valued all scholarships at about $2.8 billion that fall. For post 5.
- **Kindergartners.** The 22,294 new kindergarten students had no prior school, so the record can't say where they would have gone.
- **Public enrollment.** Public school enrollment was 2,870,527 in 2022-23 and 2,859,600 in 2024-25 (NCES). For post 7.
- **Private school growth.** 30,515 more private school students and 236 more private schools in 2024-25 than in 2023-24. (FL DOE Private Schools Annual Report.)
