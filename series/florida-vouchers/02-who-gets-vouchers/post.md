Who Gets Florida's Vouchers, and Where Were They Before?

Florida's oldest voucher program still running was built for poor children leaving public schools. In 2023, the first year vouchers were open to every child, most new recipients were children already in private school.

Florida calls its vouchers "scholarships." As the first post in this series described, two programs remain today. The Tax Credit Scholarship began in 2001. Companies donate to nonprofit groups that award the scholarships, and the state gives the companies a tax credit for the donation. The Family Empowerment Scholarship began in 2019. The state pays for it through its school funding formula. It is now the larger of the two.

In the Tax Credit Scholarship's early years, a child had to meet two rules. The family had to be poor enough for free or reduced-price school lunch. The child also had to be leaving a public school or starting kindergarten or first grade. That is according to OPPAGA, the Florida Legislature's research office.

A 2009 study for the state Department of Education showed that the program mostly served poor children from public schools. The author, David N. Figlio, an economist then at the University of Florida, looked at students who joined in 2007-08. He compared them with low-income children who stayed in public school. The scholarship students were poorer. They had lower test scores. More of them came from schools the state rated poorly.

In March 2023, the Legislature passed House Bill 1. It made any child who could enroll in a Florida public school eligible for a scholarship. The child did not need to attend a public school. A child already in private school qualified, whatever the family's income.

Before the vote, the staff of a Florida House budget committee estimated how many children already in private school would take a scholarship in the first year. Their answer was about 58,000.

In September 2023, Step Up For Students, the nonprofit that runs most of Florida's scholarships, counted the new students. There were 122,895. Of those, 84,505, or 69 percent, had been in private school. Another 22,294 were starting kindergarten. Only 16,096, or 13 percent, came from public schools.

Each side pointed to a different number. Step Up, which supports the program, pointed to the 16,096 students who left public schools. Its report argued that this was far from the "exodus" from public schools that critics had forecast.

The Florida Policy Institute, a research group that opposed the law, pointed to the 84,505 students already in private school. It said the "money is going to subsidize tuition that families were already paying."

Both numbers are correct. In its first year, the law moved few children out of public schools. Most new scholarships went to children who were already in private school, whose families had been paying for it without a state scholarship.

The children who do leave public schools have changed, too. A 2025 study for the state by Florida State University looked at students who left public schools in 2023-24 for one of the state's scholarships. They were less likely to be poor than the students who stayed. Of the students who left, 42 percent qualified for free or reduced-price lunch. Of those who stayed, 52 percent did. Test scores were about the same for both groups. The state study did not say why. In a 2025 survey by Step Up, families who won a scholarship but did not use it most often cited no open seat at the school they wanted, tuition or fees beyond the scholarship, and transportation.

In 2007-08, a typical new scholarship student was a poor child leaving a public school. In 2023, a typical new scholarship student was already in a private school. A later post looks at what that change costs the state. The next post looks at two other ways families leave traditional public schools: charter schools, which are public but run independently, and home schooling.

Sources are in the first comment.

**Caveats:**
- The 69 percent figure comes from Step Up For Students, which administers the scholarships and supports them, and reached us through a news report. No state agency has published the same breakdown that we found. The figures were not disputed by critics, who cited them.
- The Step Up count was taken Sept. 8, 2023, early in the school year. The House estimate covered the full year and only income-based scholarships, so the two are not strictly comparable; the post does not compare them directly.
- Whether the Auditor General's Tax Credit count for 2024-25 includes home-education (PEP) students is not stated in what we have read. The post says "some students taught at home" to stay safe. To confirm.
- "Typical" in the closing line rests on the 69 percent (2023) and on the early eligibility rule plus Figlio (2007-08).

## Notes: details kept out of the main text

- **Why poorer children don't use scholarships more: what we know.** The 2025 FSU evaluation reports the income gap but gives no reason for it. The closest evidence is a survey by Step Up For Students (Ron Matus and Dava Cherry, "Going with Plan B," July 2025) of families awarded a scholarship for 2024-25 who did not use it: about 41,000 such families; 32,000 emailed; 2,739 responses. Reasons: no available seat at the preferred school (34.7%), scholarship (about $8,000) did not cover tuition (19.7%), worry about fees beyond tuition (19.6%), lack of transportation (just under 10%), school too far (5.8%). 40% of respondents who reported income were below 185% of the poverty level. Source here is Fordham Institute commentary by Jeff Murray, July 31, 2025 (https://fordhaminstitute.org/national/commentary/why-tens-thousands-florida-families-didnt-use-their-private-school-scholarships); the Step Up report itself still to read. Limits: an advocate's survey, low response rate, not broken down by income, and about families who were awarded but didn't enroll, not about who applies.
- **Why a recipient's previous school matters to the state's costs (moved from draft 4, for post 5).** The state already pays for every public school student through the school funding formula. If a public school student takes a scholarship, the state pays for the scholarship instead. If a child already in private school takes one, the state pays for a child it was not paying for before. House PreK-12 Appropriations Subcommittee staff, in their February 2023 analysis of HB 1, counted no new cost for students leaving public schools and counted private school students as new spending (pp. 20-21).
- **Which program is bigger.** The Tax Credit Scholarship was the largest program from 2007-08 to 2021-22. Family Empowerment became larger in 2022-23 and in 2024-25 had 382,841 students to Tax Credit's 114,443 (Auditor General 2026-046). The post avoids "largest" to stay consistent with post 1.
- **Family income, fall 2023 (moved from draft 1).** Of all scholarship students that fall, 44 percent came from households below 185 percent of the federal poverty level (about $55,000 for a family of four), 29 percent from 185 to 400 percent, and 27 percent above 400 percent (about $120,000) or did not report income. (Step Up, via News Service of Florida.)
- **Enrollment counts, 2022-23 to 2024-25 (moved from draft 1).** Scholarship students rose by 230,581 (Auditor General 2026-046); private school enrollment rose by 51,452 (FL DOE annual survey). The counts are measured differently: the scholarship count includes some home-education (PEP) students and anyone funded during the year; the survey is a snapshot. The gap hints that much of the growth came from families already paying for private school, but it is our arithmetic, not an official finding. Possible material for post 5.
- **The two studies used different comparison groups.** Figlio compared participants with public school students who received subsidized meals; the FSU study compared them with public school students generally, because after HB 1 everyone was eligible. FSU also excluded students with disabilities eligible for McKay and covered only the 8,956 new students it could link to public school records.
- **House staff estimate vs Step Up count.** The staff expected 58,315 private school students in the full year, in the income-based programs. Step Up counted 84,505 by Sept. 8. Not strictly comparable (timing, and whether Step Up's count includes disability scholarships), so the post doesn't set them side by side.
- **When the prior-public-school rule ended.** OPPAGA documents it in 2008. Later laws loosened it before HB 1; the exact year is not yet verified. Before HB 1, the Family Empowerment rule barred students currently enrolled in public school, and students who spent the prior year in public school were not counted against the program's cap. (House PKA analysis pp. 10-11.)
- **Income limits just before HB 1.** By 2022-23, the Tax Credit and Family Empowerment income cap had risen to 400 percent of the poverty level ($111,000 for a family of four), with priority for families at or below 185 percent. (House PKA analysis pp. 3, 10-11.) HB 1 kept the 185 percent priority. Post 1 says the 2021 law set 375 percent; the adjusted cap reached 400 percent by 2022-23. Consistent, but worth knowing if asked.
- **Race in 2007-08.** New Tax Credit participants were more likely to be Black (46.9 vs 34.2 percent) and less likely to be Hispanic or white. (Figlio 2009, Table IV.1.) In 2023-24, new Family Empowerment students from public schools were 18.7 percent Black vs 20.1 percent of those who stayed; 32.6 vs 37.8 percent Hispanic. (FSU LSI, Figure 6.)
- **English learners.** 15.3 percent of new Family Empowerment students from public schools vs 23.7 percent of those who stayed. (FSU LSI, Figure 6.)
- **Scale, fall 2023.** 242,929 students using Tax Credit and Family Empowerment scholarships in 2,098 private schools as of Sept. 8, 2023; 15,097 home-education (PEP) students. (Step Up, via News Service of Florida.)
- **Cost estimates in 2023.** The House's final analysis put the bill's first-year state cost at $209.6 million. FPI estimated $676 million of the expansion would go to students already in private school, and Step Up valued all scholarships at about $2.8 billion that fall. For post 5.
- **Kindergartners.** The 22,294 new kindergarten students had no prior school, so the record can't say where they would have gone.
- **Public enrollment.** Public school enrollment was 2,870,527 in 2022-23 and 2,859,600 in 2024-25 (NCES). For post 7.
- **Private school growth.** 30,515 more private school students and 236 more private schools in 2024-25 than in 2023-24. (FL DOE Private Schools Annual Report.)
