## Post text (paste as is)

Florida vouchers, 2 of 12

Who Gets Florida's Vouchers, and Where Were They Before?

Florida's oldest voucher program still running was built for poor children leaving public schools. In 2023, the first year vouchers were open to every child, most new recipients were already in private school.

Florida calls its vouchers "scholarships." As the first post in this series described, two programs remain, and they offer four kinds of aid. The Tax Credit Scholarship, paid from donations that earn companies a tax credit, pays for private school or, since 2023, for home schooling through an account. The Family Empowerment Scholarship, paid through the state's school funding formula, has a general scholarship for private school and a disability scholarship. Most of this post is about the two scholarships for private school that any child can get: the Tax Credit scholarship and the general Family Empowerment scholarship.

In the Tax Credit Scholarship's early years, a child had to come from a family poor enough for free or reduced-price school lunch, and had to be leaving a public school or starting kindergarten or first grade, according to OPPAGA, the Legislature's research office.

The program mostly served poor children from public schools. In a 2009 study for the state Department of Education, David N. Figlio, an economist then at the University of Florida, compared students who joined in 2007-08 with low-income children who stayed in public school. The scholarship students were poorer, had lower test scores, and more often came from schools the state rated poorly.

In March 2023, the Legislature passed House Bill 1. It made any child who could enroll in a Florida public school eligible for a scholarship, whatever the family's income. The child did not need to attend one. Children already in private school qualified.

The programs grew quickly. The state's auditor general reported 266,703 scholarship students of all kinds in 2022-23, the last year before the law took effect, and 398,185 in 2023-24.

Most of the 2023-24 students already had a scholarship. In September 2023, Step Up For Students, the nonprofit that runs most of Florida's scholarships, counted the students who were new that fall: 122,895. Of those, 84,505, or 69 percent, had been in private school. Another 22,294 were starting kindergarten. Only 16,096, or 13 percent, came from public schools.

Supporters and opponents of the law pointed to different numbers. Step Up, which supports the program, pointed to the 16,096 who left public schools, far from the "exodus" it said critics had forecast. The Florida Policy Institute, a research group that opposed the law, pointed to the 84,505 already in private school. It said the "money is going to subsidize tuition that families were already paying."

Both numbers are correct. In its first year, the law moved few children out of public schools. Most new scholarships went to children whose families had been paying for private school without a state scholarship. In the terms of the money map in the first post, money moved more than children did: tuition that families had paid themselves was now paid by a scholarship.

The children who do leave public schools have changed, too. A 2025 evaluation by Florida State University, required by state law, looked at students who left public schools for a Family Empowerment Scholarship in 2023-24. They were less likely to be poor than those who stayed: 42 percent qualified for free or reduced-price lunch, against 52 percent. Test scores were about the same. The study did not say why. In a 2025 Step Up survey, families who won a scholarship but did not use it most often cited no open seat at the school they wanted, tuition or fees beyond the scholarship, and transportation.

The disability scholarship follows its own rules. It has no income test. A diagnosis from a doctor or psychologist qualifies a child, without a special-education plan from a school, according to a 2025 Florida Senate staff analysis. About 16 percent of Florida's public school students have a disability, according to the Department of Education. The scholarship grew from 74,446 students in 2022-23, according to the auditor general, to 163,242 in 2025-26, according to Senate staff, about three in ten of all scholarship students that year. The law caps the number of students, but children leaving public schools do not count against the cap, and lawmakers lifted it for 2023-24 when demand passed it.

In 2007-08, a typical new scholarship student was a poor child leaving a public school. In 2023, a typical new one was already in private school. A later post looks at what that change costs the state. The next post looks at where Florida's children learn, and at two other ways families leave district-run public schools: charter schools, which are public but run independently, and home schooling.

Sources are in the first comment.

## Notes: details kept out of the main text

- **Why poorer children don't use scholarships more: what we know.** The 2025 FSU evaluation reports the income gap but gives no reason for it. The closest evidence is a survey by Step Up For Students (Ron Matus and Dava Cherry, "Going with Plan B," July 2025) of families awarded a scholarship for 2024-25 who did not use it: about 41,000 such families; 32,000 emailed; 2,739 responses. Reasons: no available seat at the preferred school (34.7%), scholarship (about $8,000) did not cover tuition (19.7%), worry about fees beyond tuition (19.6%), lack of transportation (just under 10%), school too far (5.8%). 40% of respondents who reported income were below 185% of the poverty level. Source: Fordham Institute commentary by Jeff Murray, July 31, 2025 (source 9); Step Up's report itself was not found online. Limits: an advocate's survey, low response rate, not broken down by income, and about families who were awarded but didn't enroll, not about who applies.
- **Why a recipient's previous school matters to the state's costs (moved from draft 4, for post 5).** The state already pays for every public school student through the school funding formula. If a public school student takes a scholarship, the state pays for the scholarship instead. If a child already in private school takes one, the state pays for a child it was not paying for before. House PreK-12 Appropriations Subcommittee staff, in their February 2023 analysis of HB 1, counted no new cost for students leaving public schools and counted private school students as new spending (pp. 20-21).
- **Which program is bigger.** The Tax Credit Scholarship was the largest program from 2007-08 (21,493 students vs McKay's 19,852) to 2020-21. Counting both of its parts, Family Empowerment became larger in 2021-22 (about 107,500 vs 85,612; project spreadsheet from Auditor General and House analysis figures), and in 2024-25 had 382,841 students to Tax Credit's 114,443 (Auditor General 2026-046). The post avoids "largest" to stay consistent with post 1.
- **Family income, fall 2023 (moved from draft 1).** Of all scholarship students that fall, 44 percent came from households below 185 percent of the federal poverty level (about $55,000 for a family of four), 29 percent from 185 to 400 percent, and 27 percent above 400 percent (about $120,000) or did not report income. (Step Up, via News Service of Florida.)
- **Enrollment counts, 2022-23 to 2024-25 (moved from draft 1).** Scholarship students rose by 230,581, from 266,703 to 497,284 (Auditor General 2026-046). Private school enrollment rose by 51,452, from 445,067 to 496,519 (FL DOE, "Florida's Private Schools, 2024-25 School Year Annual Report," https://www.fldoe.org/core/fileparse.php/7562/urlt/PS-AnnualReport2025.pdf). The counts are measured differently: the auditor general's figures are undefined department records and may include home-education (PEP) students; the survey is a snapshot. The gap hints that much of the growth came from families already paying for private school, but it is our arithmetic, not an official finding. Possible material for post 5.
- **The two studies used different comparison groups.** Figlio compared participants with public school students who received subsidized meals; the FSU study compared them with public school students generally, because after HB 1 everyone was eligible. FSU also excluded students with disabilities eligible for McKay and covered only the 8,956 new students it could link to public school records.
- **House staff estimate (cut from the text in draft 8).** Before the vote, House PreK-12 Appropriations Subcommittee staff estimated 58,315 private school students would take a new scholarship in 2023-24.
- **House staff estimate vs Step Up count.** The staff expected 58,315 private school students in the full year, in the income-based programs. Step Up counted 84,505 by Sept. 8. Not strictly comparable (timing, and whether Step Up's count includes disability scholarships), so the post doesn't set them side by side.
- **When the prior-public-school rule ended.** OPPAGA documents it in 2008. The House's 2023 analysis of HB 1 lists no prior-public-school requirement for the Tax Credit Scholarship, so it ended at some point before 2023; the year is not yet verified. Before HB 1, the Family Empowerment rule barred students currently enrolled in public school, and students who spent the prior year in public school were not counted against the program's cap. (House PKA analysis pp. 10-11.)
- **Income limits just before HB 1.** By 2022-23, the Tax Credit and Family Empowerment income cap had risen to 400 percent of the poverty level ($111,000 for a family of four), with priority for families at or below 185 percent. (House PKA analysis pp. 3, 10-11.) HB 1 kept the 185 percent priority. Post 1 says the 2021 law set 375 percent; the adjusted cap reached 400 percent by 2022-23. Consistent, but worth knowing if asked.
- **Race in 2007-08.** New Tax Credit participants were more likely to be Black (46.9 vs 34.2 percent) and less likely to be Hispanic or white. (Figlio 2009, Table IV.1.) In 2023-24, new Family Empowerment students from public schools were 18.7 percent Black vs 20.1 percent of those who stayed; 32.6 vs 37.8 percent Hispanic. (FSU LSI, Figure 6.)
- **English learners.** 15.3 percent of new Family Empowerment students from public schools vs 23.7 percent of those who stayed. (FSU LSI, Figure 6.)
- **Scale, fall 2023.** 242,929 students using Tax Credit and Family Empowerment scholarships in 2,098 private schools as of Sept. 8, 2023; 15,097 home-education (PEP) students. (Step Up, via News Service of Florida.)
- **Cost estimates in 2023.** The House's final analysis put the bill's first-year state cost at $209.6 million. FPI estimated $676 million of the expansion would go to students already in private school, and Step Up valued all scholarships at about $2.8 billion that fall. For post 5.
- **Kindergartners.** The 22,294 new kindergarten students had no prior school, so the record can't say where they would have gone.
- **Public enrollment.** Public school enrollment was 2,870,527 in 2022-23 and 2,859,600 in 2024-25 (NCES Common Core of Data, via the project spreadsheet; re-verify the 2022-23 figure before use). For post 7.
- **Private school growth.** 30,515 more private school students and 236 more private schools in 2024-25 than in 2023-24. (FL DOE Private Schools Annual Report.)

- **The auditor's Tax Credit count includes the home-schooling account (added 25 Oct).** Senate staff list 54,356 Tax Credit and 58,943 account students as of March 2025 (SB 7030 analysis, p. 2); together 113,299, close to the auditor's 114,443. This corrects the earlier note that the report doesn't say: it doesn't say, but the numbers show it. By fall 2025, only 7,560 children used Tax Credit at a private school (CS/SB 318 analysis, p. 3).
