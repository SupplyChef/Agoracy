## Post text (paste as is)

Florida vouchers, 1 of 12

How Florida Went From Vouchers for Failing Schools to Vouchers for Everyone

Over 25 years, the state created five programs. Two remain. They are open to every child, and they are worth nearly $4 billion a year.

In 1999, Florida paid $3,525 to send two children with disabilities to private school, according to a Florida Senate report. They were the first students in the McKay Scholarship, a pilot run in a single school district. It let a public school student with a disability take state school funding to a private school or to another public school.

That same year, the Legislature passed a second program with a different aim. Proposed by Gov. Jeb Bush, the Opportunity Scholarship offered a voucher to any child assigned to a public school the state had rated as failing in two of four years.

Florida calls this aid "scholarships." In other states it would be called vouchers or, in some cases, education savings accounts. By the 2024-25 school year, the programs were funding 497,284 students, worth $3.98 billion, according to the state's auditor general. That is about one scholarship student for every six students in Florida's public schools, at an average of about $8,000 each. Here is how it got there.

In 2000, lawmakers took the McKay pilot statewide. It grew from 1,021 students that school year to 9,130 two years later, according to the same Senate report.

In 2001, they added a third program, funded in a new way. Under the Tax Credit Scholarship, corporations donate to nonprofit scholarship organizations and receive a state tax credit equal to the donation. Eligibility turned on income alone. Any child from a family poor enough to qualify for free or reduced-price school lunch could apply, if the child was leaving a public school or starting kindergarten or first grade. There was no disability or failing-school requirement.

The voucher for failing schools did not last. In January 2006, the Florida Supreme Court ruled 5 to 2 that its private-school option violated the State Constitution, which requires "a uniform, efficient, safe, secure, and high quality system of free public schools." The majority read that clause as making public schools the way the state must educate children, and found that voucher schools were held to different rules: they could hire teachers without bachelor's degrees and did not have to teach the subjects public schools must. Chief Justice Barbara J. Pariente wrote that the program "diverts public dollars into separate private systems parallel to and in competition with the free public schools." In dissent, Justice Kenneth B. Bell wrote that the Constitution "does not forbid the Legislature from enacting a well-delineated program" of this kind.

The ruling ended the voucher for failing schools, but the case involved only that program. The other two kept growing.

In 2014, lawmakers created a fourth program, the Gardiner Scholarship, a spending account for children with disabilities.

In 2019 came the fifth, the Family Empowerment Scholarship. Unlike the Tax Credit program, it was paid through the Florida Education Finance Program, the formula that sets how much money each school district receives per student. Each scholarship was set at 95 percent of what the formula provided for a student in the basic program. Whether that money comes out of public schools' share, or adds to the total, is disputed; a later post in this series takes it up. The program was aimed at middle- and lower-income families, those earning up to 300 percent of the federal poverty level, and was capped at 18,000 students.

In 2021, the Legislature began merging the programs. It folded the two disability programs into Family Empowerment, Gardiner that year and McKay the next. The same law raised the income limit for the two income-based programs, Tax Credit and Family Empowerment, to 375 percent of the poverty level.

That left two programs. The Florida Tax Credit Scholarship was open to children from families under the income limit and was paid for by corporate donations the state credits against taxes. The Family Empowerment Scholarship had two parts: one for children from families under the same income limit, and one for children with disabilities, with no income test. It was paid with state money through the school funding formula.

In March 2023, the Legislature removed the income limits. House Bill 1 passed 83 to 27 in the House and 26 to 12 in the Senate. It made every child eligible to attend a Florida public school eligible for a scholarship, whatever the family's income. Families apply through a nonprofit scholarship organization, and since 2021 the award has been the same in both income-based programs. What differs is who pays. The law requires every Tax Credit scholarship to be funded, from donations, before income-based Family Empowerment scholarships are awarded from the state budget. Gov. Ron DeSantis signed it on March 27.

Its Senate sponsor, Sen. Corey Simon, put the argument for it plainly: "we are going to stop funding systems and start funding students." The Florida Education Association, the statewide teachers union, said the law "will drain billions of taxpayer dollars away from the neighborhood public schools."

Within two years, enrollment nearly doubled. The auditor general counted 266,703 scholarship students and $2.0 billion in 2022-23, and 497,284 students and $3.98 billion in 2024-25.

No single vote created Florida's universal system. Each of the five programs began with limited eligibility, defined by disability, family income or a school's rating. Over 24 years, the limits were widened, the programs were merged, and finally the limits were removed.

Supporters see that path as lawmakers responding, step by step, to parents who wanted more options. Critics see a program sold as a rescue for children in failing schools that became something much larger.

What the record does not show is how many of today's scholarship students would have gone to private school anyway. That question is central to what the programs cost, and it is the subject of the next post.

Sources are in the first comment.

## Notes: details kept out of the main text

These are accurate and sourced. They were moved out of the post because they aren't needed to follow how the programs grew. Use them in replies to comments, or in later posts.

- **The school funding formula (FEFP), briefly.** Florida funds districts through the Florida Education Finance Program. It counts students, weights them by program, and multiplies by a base amount per student ($5,372.60 in 2025-26) and a regional cost factor. The state pays part of the total, and the rest comes from a property tax rate the state requires each district to levy (Required Local Effort, about $10.9 billion statewide in 2025-26). Family Empowerment scholarships are calculated inside the formula and shown as an "Adjustment for Family Empowerment Scholarships." (FL DOE, *Funding for Florida School Districts 2025-26*, https://cdn.fldoe.org/file/7507/Fefpdist.pdf; FEFP 2024-25 Final Calculation.) Full explanation in post 5.
- **How the two programs work together.** Under HB 1, income-based Family Empowerment scholarships are awarded only after all Tax Credit scholarships are funded, so donated money is used first. (Senate summary of HB 1.) On Step Up For Students' 2025-26 application, the family picks a program and may apply to only one at a time; Family Empowerment requires the student's Social Security number, and an applicant without one is considered only for Tax Credit. (Step Up training document, a description of the administrator's process, not a legal source: https://go.stepupforstudents.org/hubfs/CR%20External%20Training%202024/_FTC%20FES-EO%20PEP%20New%20Application%20%2025-26%20SY.pdf.) HB 1 also added a second priority group for household incomes between 185 and 400 percent of the poverty level. For the family, the program mostly doesn't matter; for the state, it decides whether the cost is forgone tax revenue or budget spending (post 5). Why Florida keeps two programs isn't stated in any record found so far. A likely reason is that it lets the state draw on both corporate tax credits and the school budget, but that is inference. To research for post 5.
- **Tax credits as lost revenue.** Of the $3.98 billion, $804.5 million is Tax Credit scholarships. They are funded by corporate donations the state credits against taxes, so their cost shows up as forgone revenue rather than spending, and it never appears in the budget. (Auditor General 2026-046, Table 4; OPPAGA 08-68.) Save this for post 5.
- **McKay was also paid from the public school formula.** "The program is funded through the Florida Education Finance Program." (Senate Interim Report 2004-130.) So Family Empowerment was not the first program paid that way. It was the first income-based one.
- **Gardiner enrolled 1,559 students in its first year,** 2014-15. (FL DOE Gardiner Fast Facts.)
- **How Family Empowerment money moves.** Since 2021, the award is 100 percent of the amount calculated for the student in the formula, paid with state funds only. The Department of Education transfers it to the scholarship organization for quarterly payments, and may not make an award that would exceed 99 percent of the school district's share of state funds. Since HB 1 (2023), the department, not districts, reports scholarship students for funding. (Senate summaries of HB 7045 and HB 1.) Whether this reduces what public schools get or adds to the total is post 5's question.
- **Tax Credit eligibility detail.** Renewing students also stayed eligible. Free or reduced-price lunch is a federal standard, not a Florida one. (OPPAGA 08-68.)
- **McKay award size:** average $5,840 in 2002-03, from $2,205 to $21,326. (Senate Interim Report 2004-130.)
- **Under HB 1, Tax Credit also funds a home-education account** (the Personalized Education Program). Post 3 covers it. (Senate summary of HB 1.)
- **Tax Credit growth after the ruling:** 11,550 students in 2003-04, 21,493 in 2007-08. (OPPAGA 08-68.)
- **The 2024-25 split:** Family Empowerment 382,841 students and $3.17 billion (Educational Options 267,413 and $2.00 billion; Unique Abilities, the disability part, 115,428 and $1.17 billion). Tax Credit 114,443 students and $0.80 billion. (Auditor General 2026-046, Tables 2-4.)
- **DeSantis's signing quote:** "the largest expansion of education choice in the history of these United States." He signed the bill in Miami. (Governor's press release; WLRN.)
- **Other voices in the governor's release:** House Speaker Paul Renner ("Florida puts parents and students first by giving them the freedom to customize their education") and Senate President Kathleen Passidomo.
