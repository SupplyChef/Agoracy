What Florida's Vouchers Cost, and Where the Money Comes From

In 2008, the Legislature's auditors found that a Florida voucher program saved the state money, as long as most of its students would otherwise have been in public school. In 2023, the first year vouchers were open to every child, most new recipients came from private schools.

In 2007-08, the Tax Credit Scholarship paid private school tuition for 21,493 children, at up to $3,750 each. The state's school funding formula provided $6,106 for each public school student. OPPAGA, the Legislature's research office, estimated that the program cost $79.2 million in lost corporate tax revenue and saved $118.1 million in school funding, for net savings of $38.9 million.

The savings rested on one assumption. OPPAGA estimated that 90 percent of the students would have attended public school without a scholarship. At the time, new recipients had to come from a public school unless they were starting kindergarten or first grade. If only 60 percent would have attended public school, the report said, the savings disappear.

The reasoning is simple. The state already pays for each public school student. When one leaves with a scholarship, the state pays for the scholarship instead. When a child already in private school takes one, the state pays for a child it was not paying for before.

In 2019, Florida created the Family Empowerment Scholarship, paid through the formula that funds school districts. In 2023, House Bill 1 opened both programs to every child. Before the vote, House staff estimated the first-year cost at $209.6 million. They counted no new cost for public school students who switched, because the formula already paid for them, and expected 58,315 private school students to take a new scholarship.

That fall, 69 percent of new scholarship students had been in private school, according to Step Up For Students, the nonprofit that runs most of the scholarships. Scholarship funding rose from $2.0 billion in 2022-23 to $3.98 billion in 2024-25, according to the state's auditor general.

Two kinds of money pay for it. Tax Credit scholarships, $0.8 billion in 2024-25, come from corporate donations the state credits against taxes, so their cost is revenue never collected and does not appear in the budget. Family Empowerment scholarships, $3.2 billion, come from the state's share of the formula.

In 2024-25, that share ran short. The state had budgeted $2.79 billion for Family Empowerment and spent $3.19 billion, about $400 million more, the auditor general reported. At the end of the year, the Department of Education cut districts' formula funding by $250 million to stay within the appropriation, offset in part by $97 million from an enrollment stabilization fund. Under state law, only district funding is cut in a shortfall, not scholarships, according to Florida Senate staff. The net cut, about $153 million, was about half of 1 percent of the $28.6 billion in state and local school funding. Such cuts predate the universal program: districts lost $37 million in 2019-20.

The larger change is in what the state's money pays for. From 2022-23 to 2024-25, state money in the formula rose from $13.6 billion to $15.6 billion, according to the department's final calculations. Family Empowerment scholarships took $1.86 billion of the $1.99 billion increase, and the state money left for public schools rose $129 million, to $12.4 billion. In 2025-26, state money held at $15.6 billion while scholarships rose to $3.9 billion, and the state money left for public schools fell $743 million. Some of that money pays for children who left public schools and would otherwise be counted there; no state record says how much. Local property taxes for schools rose over the same years, which the next post takes up. All dollars here are nominal, not adjusted for inflation.

In January 2026, the Florida Senate voted 38 to 0 for a bill by Sen. Don Gaetz, a Republican, to pay for Family Empowerment from its own fund, separate from district funding, as the auditor general had recommended. Quoting the auditor general, Sen. Gaetz said "whatever could go wrong has gone wrong." He added: "If this issue is left to fester, it will imperil school choice in our state." The House did not take up the bill.

The two sides read the cost differently. Step Up says in a fact sheet that "every study shows the scholarships save state tax money," and compares scholarships of $7,250 to $7,850 in 2022-23 with $8,217 in formula funding for each public school student. The studies it cites predate 2023, when most recipients had to come from public schools. Sadaf Knight, chief executive of the Florida Policy Institute, a research group that opposed House Bill 1, said in January 2025 that the cost of vouchers continues "to balloon to the detriment of public schools."

Supporters see a scholarship that costs less than a public school seat, so each child who switches saves money. Critics see a new cost for children who were already in private school, paid from the same pot as public schools.

In 2008, the state's auditors said the program saved money if at least 60 percent of its students would otherwise have been in public school. The state has not published a new estimate of that share since House Bill 1. The next post asks what these changes mean for public schools' money per student, and who is making up the difference.

Sources are in the first comment.

**Caveats:**
- The Auditor General and the Final Calculation describe the 2024-25 shortfall with different figures. The Auditor General says a May 2025 budget amendment released $118.0 million from the stabilization program and the 24th and final payment was still reduced by $47 million (Exhibit B: $47,099,007); a further $47,117,395 was released on Nov. 6, 2025 to meet "outstanding obligations." The Final Calculation (Nov. 5, 2025) shows a $250.1 million proration and a $97.3 million stabilization allocation. The post uses the Final Calculation, which Senate staff also cite. To reconcile before posting.
- The Auditor General links the shortfall to "unexpected funding demand" and says scholarship participation "significantly exceeded expectations," but also cites delays in surveys, calculations and cross-checks. The post does not say scholarships alone caused the cut.
- The Gaetz quotes were read through a summary of the release; confirm the exact wording against the PDF before posting.
- Whether the 2026 budget changed where scholarships sit in the formula was not confirmed: the Senate's May 2026 proposal (SB 2500-E as introduced) showed a separate "Scholarship Funding Allocation" of $4.48 billion, but the department's July calculation shows the usual adjustment.

## Notes: details kept out of the main text

- **OPPAGA's 2010 update.** For 2008-09, OPPAGA estimated savings of $36.2 million, assuming 95 percent of recipients would otherwise have attended public school (OPPAGA research memorandum, March 1, 2010, https://www.fldoe.org/core/fileparse.php/5423/urlt/OPPAGA_March_2010_Report.pdf). The memo gives no break-even share; our arithmetic from its figures ($82.2 million in forgone taxes against $5,872 x 21,219 = $124.6 million in potential savings) puts it near 66 percent. Inference, not OPPAGA's figure.
- **Our estimate of the new cost for 2023-24 (not used).** 84,505 students already in private school x the 2023-24 average award of about $7,420 (all scholarship dollars / all scholarship students, Auditor General) = about $627 million a year. Rough: the average includes disability scholarships and home-schooling accounts. FPI estimated $676 million for the same group (post 2 notes). Neither is a state estimate.
- **House estimate vs Step Up count.** House staff expected 58,315 private school students in the full year in the general scholarship; Step Up counted 84,505 by Sept. 8, 2023, possibly including other scholarships. Not strictly comparable (post 2 notes).
- **Administrative fees.** Scholarship organizations may keep up to 3 percent of Tax Credit scholarships and up to 2.5 percent of Family Empowerment scholarships (CS/SB 318 analysis, p. 23). Applied to 2024-25 payments, that is a ceiling of about $24 million and $79 million (our arithmetic: $804.5M x 3%; $3,173.8M x 2.5%). The 2026 bill would have cut the rates to 2 and 1.5 percent, freeing about $50 million (p. 25). For post 10.
- **The auditor general's other money findings.** Department recoupments for 2024-25 totaled "nearly $51 million"; the department "could not evidence the reconciliation" of requested returns of funds (pp. 13-14). For post 9.
- **Students counted twice.** The department identified "potential duplicate students enrolled in both the scholarship program and public school" but relied on voluntary parent surveys (CS/SB 318 analysis, p. 5). Sen. Gaetz, in a podcast interview reported by a blog, said "Last year we couldn't find 23,000 students on any given day. This year we can't find 30,000." Not used: secondary source. For post 9.
- **FTE above projection.** In 2024-25, unweighted FTE was "over 9,100 greater" and weighted FTE "nearly 45,000 greater" than the March 2024 projection, "representing a significant unexpected draw on State education funding" (CS/SB 318 analysis, p. 5).
- **2026-27 set-aside (cut from the text in draft 1).** The 2026-27 Second Calculation (July 16, 2026) shows an "Adjustment for Family Empowerment Scholarships" of $4,526,653,050, about $4.5 billion. Not a final figure.
- **2026-27 forecast.** The April 2025 Education Estimating Conference forecast 570,325 scholarships for 2026-27; Senate staff priced them at about $4.99 billion, including Tax Credit (CS/SB 318 analysis, p. 27). The Aug. 11, 2026 conference projects 482,528 Family Empowerment FTE for 2026-27 (p. 5).
- **Discretionary supplement.** The 2025-26 and 2026-27 calculations add back a "State-Funded Discretionary Supplement" ($862.9 million and $1.0 billion). It sits inside Total State Funding, so it does not change the figures in the post. What it pays for is to check before post 6.
- **Other ways of measuring the share.** FPI says the voucher share of state formula dollars rose from 12 percent in 2021-22 to 23 percent in 2024-25 (release, Jan. 14, 2025). On Total State Funding, our figures are 9.6 percent (2022-23), 20.4 percent (2024-25) and 25.1 percent (2025-26). FPI's base is not stated; the difference is probably the base.
- **Sen. Simon in 2023.** On the Senate floor: "My Democratic colleagues would not have one problem, if this bill was $650 million or $6 billion" (Florida Phoenix, March 23, 2023). News only; not used.
- **Other critics' voices.** Andrew Spar, FEA: "Our public schools are struggling to make ends meet" (WUSF, Nov. 30, 2025). Not used: it restates the post 1 FEA quote's point.
