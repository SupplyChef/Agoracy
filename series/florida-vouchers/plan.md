# Florida's voucher programs: a 12-post Facebook series for a skeptical legislator

**Planning draft, revised 19 Oct 2026.** Topics only; no posts written yet.

**Reader:** a legislator in another state who doubts vouchers, or doubts the claims made for and against them, and who will be asked to vote on something similar. Their staff and constituents will see the posts too.

**Tone:** even-handed. Each post gives the strongest case from supporters and from critics, says who produced each number (government, independent researcher, advocacy group), and ends with what the evidence can't settle. The last two posts move from evidence to values; there the posts set out the competing arguments fairly instead of reaching a verdict.

**Facebook format for every post**
- **Hook:** the opening line is the question, with one surprising number. Only the first two or three lines show before "See more".
- **Length:** about 300-500 words, in short paragraphs, with no tables in the text.
- **One image:** a single chart or stat card per post (1080×1080 or 1200×630), readable on a phone, with the source printed on the image.
- **Body:** the short answer; "What supporters point to" and "What critics point to"; "What we don't know"; one line headed "If your state is considering this".
- **Sources:** two or three named sources in the post. The full links go in the first comment, since Facebook shows posts with outside links to fewer people.
- **Series tag** on every post, such as "Florida vouchers, 4 of 12", and a closing question to invite comments.

**Key:** ✅ we have the evidence (in the project spreadsheet (florida_school_funding_enrollment_1994-2026.xlsx, not in this repo) or the earlier findings) · 🔍 research gap to fill before writing.

## The series

### Part 1. What Florida built and who uses it

**1. Florida's 25-year experiment: from a narrow program to vouchers for everyone**
*Question:* How did a small program for students in failing schools become a universal entitlement, and why should another state care?
*Image:* timeline of programs with the student count growing from 0 to about 497K.
*Evidence:* ✅ Scale: about 497K scholarship students in 2024-25, around one in seven K-12 students, and $4.0B a year. ✅ Programs and counts: Opportunity Scholarship (1999), McKay for students with disabilities (1999/2001), Tax Credit Scholarship (2001), Gardiner ESA (2014), Family Empowerment (2019), universal eligibility (HB 1, 2023). ✅ The 2006 ruling *Bush v. Holmes*. 🔍 Legislative history and what was promised at each step. 🔍 How Florida compares with Arizona, Iowa and Utah in size and design.

**2. Who gets vouchers, and where were they before?**
*Question:* Are vouchers rescuing public school students, or paying for families already in private school?
*Image:* the split of 2023-24 new recipients: 69% already in private school, 13% from public school, 18% starting kindergarten.
*Evidence:* ✅ Before 2023, first-time recipients had to come from public school, and they were poorer and lower-scoring than eligible peers (Figlio, 2009). ✅ After 2023 the split above (Step Up For Students data). ✅ Our math: scholarships grew about 231K from 2022-23 to 2024-25 while private enrollment grew about 51K. ✅ Public enrollment peaked about 2.87M and is now falling. 🔍 Income, race and region of current recipients, and where 2024-25 onward recipients came from.

**3. Charter schools and homeschooling: the other ways out**
*Question:* How do charters and homeschooling fit alongside vouchers, and what does each teach about the others?
*Image:* enrollment over time in district, charter, voucher and home education.
*Evidence:* ✅ Home education grew from about 32K to 153K students, mostly in 2020-21. 🔍 Charter enrollment and outcomes (Stanford CREDO; FL DOE comparisons) and closures. Charters are public schools held to state tests and school grades; voucher schools are not. 🔍 The home-education ESA (Personalized Education Program, 2023): enrollment, spending, oversight. 🔍 Florida's light homeschool rules compared with other states. *Cross lessons:* what accountability can come with public money, how each option affects district enrollment, and whether the lines between charter, funded private school and funded homeschool are blurring.

**4. Religious schools: who benefits most?**
*Question:* Do religious schools receive a disproportionate share of voucher money, and does it matter?
*Image:* religious schools' share of voucher students compared with their share of private enrollment before vouchers.
*Evidence:* 🔍 Share of scholarship students and dollars in religious schools (older reports put it near 80%, to verify), by denomination, and new religious schools since 2023. 🔍 Legal background: Florida's "no aid" clause; *Espinoza* (2020) and *Carson v. Makin* (2022), which bar states from leaving religious schools out of choice programs they create. 🔍 Curriculum, such as the textbooks widely used in voucher schools that the Orlando Sentinel examined. *Both sides:* supporters say most Florida private schools were already religious, so the share reflects what was available and what parents chose; critics raise church-state concerns and public money paying for religious instruction.

### Part 2. The money

**5. What vouchers cost, and where the money comes from**
*Question:* Do vouchers save money or add a new cost, and is it carved out of public school funding?
*Image:* the state's school funding split into public schools vs vouchers, 2019-20 to 2026-27.
*Evidence:* ✅ Voucher spending $0 → $4.0B (2024-25), $4.5B budgeted for 2026-27. ✅ OPPAGA 2008 found $39M in savings, but only if about 90% of recipients would otherwise be in public school; savings vanish below about 60%. ✅ Our estimate: about $630M a year in new cost for 2023-24 recipients who were already in private school. ✅ Vouchers are paid through the school funding formula and grow automatically with sign-ups. ✅ 2019-20 to 2025-26: state school money rose $3.1B, and voucher money inside it rose $3.6B. ✅ K-12 plus vouchers held at about 14% of the state budget while the public school part fell from 15.2% to 10.2%. ✅ Tax-credit scholarships are lost tax revenue, so they never show up in the budget. 🔍 Any post-2023 cost study, Step Up's administrative fee, and audit findings.

**6. Public schools' money per student, and the property tax backstop**
*Question:* Are public schools worse off per student, and who is covering the gap?
*Image:* inflation-adjusted state vs local money per public student, 1999-00 to 2026-27.
*Evidence:* ✅ Per public student in 2024-25 dollars: $8,810 (1999-00), $10,500 peak (2007-08), $9,320 (2019-20), $8,840 (2025-26). ✅ The state's part fell about $1,180 per student since 2019-20; local property tax made up about $700. ✅ The local share rose from 38% to 54%. ✅ The 2008-11 recession dip as a comparison not caused by vouchers. ✅ 2026-27 outlook with flat property values; Amendment 3 on the November ballot. ✅ Average scholarship about $8,000, roughly 90% of public per-student money. 🔍 District-by-district ability to raise local money. 🔍 Federal and building funds, for a complete comparison.

**7. What it means for a school district**
*Question:* When students leave, can districts actually cut costs?
*Image:* where a Florida school dollar goes: instruction 53%, buildings 22%, administration 5.6%, buses 3.1%.
*Evidence:* ✅ District spending by function (2022-23). ✅ Teacher pay ranks 50th nationally. 🔍 Fixed vs variable costs. 🔍 School closures and consolidations linked to enrollment loss (Broward, Miami-Dade, Duval, Pinellas). 🔍 Costs for the students who remain, such as students with disabilities and English learners.

### Part 3. Results, rules and influence

**8. Results: for voucher students, and for the public schools they leave**
*Question:* Do voucher students learn more, graduate and go to college at higher rates, and do public schools get better or worse when students can leave?
*Image:* test scores vs college enrollment results, Florida and other states side by side.
*Evidence:* 🔍 The biggest evidence gap. Needed: Florida's required test results for Tax Credit students (FSU Learning Systems Institute), the Urban Institute college enrollment studies (Chingos and Kuehn), and any results for the universal program. 🔍 National contrast: negative early test results in Louisiana, Indiana and Ohio; more positive graduation and college results in Washington DC and Milwaukee. 🔍 Results for students with disabilities. ✅ Figlio, Hart and Karbownik (NBER): public students facing more private competition gained slightly on tests, attendance and discipline (need-based era, through 2016-17). 🔍 Earlier "threat" studies of the failing-school voucher. 🔍 The counterargument: less money per student, and easier-to-serve students may leave first. The post keeps test scores apart from graduation and college results, and need-based programs apart from universal ones.

**9. Different rules: what private schools taking public money must do, and whom they can turn away** *(expanded at RJ's request)*
*Question:* When public money follows a child to a private school, which public-school rules follow it, and which don't?
*Image:* a checklist comparing district, charter and voucher schools, row by row.
*Evidence and issues to cover:*
- 🔍 **Academic standards:** voucher schools don't take the state test or get school grades. Tax Credit students take a norm-referenced test of the school's choosing, reported in aggregate. No state-set curriculum. Curriculum content, such as the textbooks the Orlando Sentinel examined.
- 🔍 **Teacher standards:** no state teaching certificate is required; the law asks for a degree or equivalent experience (to verify in statute).
- 🔍 **Financial standards:** what voucher schools must file (surety bonds, financial reports, audits above a payment threshold) compared with public schools' public budgets and annual audits. ✅ Florida Auditor General Report 2026-046, "Funding Accountability Challenges", on payments to ineligible students and weak tracking (details to pull).
- 🔍 **Admissions:** private schools can turn students away, including on religion, disability, sexual orientation or gender identity, and many state this in their policies. The Orlando Sentinel's 2020 review of anti-LGBTQ policies at voucher schools; what Florida law does and doesn't prohibit (its rules bar race discrimination, to verify). Cross-reference post 4 on religious schools.
- 🔍 **Special-education rights:** a parent who takes a scholarship to a private school gives up a public school's federal duty to provide a free appropriate public education. How that is disclosed to parents.
- 🔍 **Documented failures:** the Sentinel's 2017 "Schools Without Rules" series, McKay fraud cases, schools closing mid-year, and the reforms that followed.
- 🔍 **Other states' rules for contrast:** states that require the state test (Louisiana, Indiana) or accreditation.

**10. Who profits, and who decides: money, influence and conflicts of interest** *(new, at RJ's request)*
*Question:* Who earns money or influence from the programs, and do the people shaping policy have a stake in it?
*Evidence and issues to cover (each claim to be documented in primary records, with the person's response included):*
- 🔍 **Scholarship organizations:** Step Up For Students administers most scholarships and keeps an administrative fee set in law (percentage to verify). Its founder's role in building the program and funding political campaigns.
- 🔍 **Erika Donalds (Florida):** former Collier County School Board member; founder of the Optima Foundation, which runs classical charter schools and has received contracts; ties to state education policy and to her husband, Rep. Byron Donalds. Sort what's documented (state records, contracts, disclosure forms) from what's alleged. Note that her business is mainly in charters, so this links to post 3.
- 🔍 **Betsy DeVos (national):** before becoming U.S. education secretary, she chaired the American Federation for Children, which funds pro-voucher campaigns, including in Florida. Her ethics agreement and divestitures on taking office. Her policies as secretary.
- 🔍 **Legislators and officials with private-school or charter interests,** found through Florida financial disclosure forms and lobbying records.
- 🔍 **Campaign money:** spending by pro-voucher groups and by teachers unions in Florida legislative races, so both sides' money is shown.
- *Shape:* the documented facts, the conflict-of-interest rules that applied, and each person's on-the-record answer. No conclusion beyond what the records show.

### Part 4. The bigger questions

**11. Educating citizens: are vouchers helping or failing the civic mission?**
*Question:* Public schools were founded partly to make citizens. Does moving students to private, religious and home settings help or hurt that?
*Image:* a quote card (Horace Mann or Jefferson) paired with one finding from the research on civic outcomes.
*Evidence:* 🔍 The founding argument: Horace Mann's common school, and Jefferson on education for self-government. 🔍 Civic outcomes research: Patrick Wolf's reviews (which mostly find private and religious schools equal or better on tolerance, voting and volunteering), the DC voucher evaluation, Cardus Education Survey, and work on sorting by school type. 🔍 Florida's civics requirements for public schools and what applies to voucher schools and homeschoolers. 🔍 *Summer for the Gods* (the Scopes trial, on who decides what is taught) and *Educated* (a homeschooled child cut off from schooling), used as illustrations and labeled as such, not as evidence of what is typical.

**12. Exit or voice: should parents leave, or stay and push for change?**
*Question:* Is it legitimate for parents to pull their children out of public school because they object to the teaching, or should they use their voice as citizens to change it?
*Image:* the exit-vs-voice idea drawn as a simple diagram.
*Evidence:* 🔍 Albert Hirschman's *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty*, and the argument that when the most engaged families leave, there is less pressure to improve. 🔍 The law on parents' rights: *Pierce v. Society of Sisters* (1925), *Wisconsin v. Yoder* (1972), *Mozert v. Hawkins* (1987), *Mahmoud v. Taylor* (2025). 🔍 Florida context: curriculum and book-removal disputes since 2021, school board races, and whether exits rose in districts with high-profile fights. 🔍 The competing views: a shared civic education (Amy Gutmann, *Democratic Education*); parents' primacy and pluralism (William Galston, Charles Glenn); and whether exit and voice really conflict, since families who leave still vote and pay school taxes. *Shape:* the strongest version of each view and the questions a legislator should ask, with no verdict. It ends the series with a question to the reader.
